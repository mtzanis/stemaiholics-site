Insight · November 2026

Students can’t always tell when AI did the thinking. Neither can an AI policy.

Stanford’s AI Hub for Education has updated its review of the rigorous research on AI and learning. One finding should change how schools think about AI policy: students can feel they learned more with AI when they did not.

The finding

Of the 1,686 papers in the AI Hub’s research database, 52 met the bar for testing what actually happens to students: randomized controlled trials and quasi-experimental studies. The review is careful about its limits. Only 10 of the 52 involved US K-12 students or teachers, and the authors say the field is still early.

Within those limits, a pattern shows. When students used AI for support, such as explaining a concept or generating practice problems, learning gains were generally better than when they used it to complete the work. Neither use led to consistent positive outcomes.

And one result runs underneath the rest. Students can think they are learning more with AI support, even when they are not.

What it looks like in a classroom

In a computer science class taught by overSTEMed’s founder, a student used AI to build a ticket generator app and was sure it worked. It did, in one sense. No compile errors. Every ticket printed. Every price was wrong.

The code ran. The thinking the task was there to build, working out how the prices should be calculated and checking that they were, never happened. From the outside the work looked finished. From the inside, the student felt it was finished too.

Why a permission rule cannot see it

Most school AI policies answer one question well: when is AI allowed? Red, amber, green. That is a real achievement, and many schools have not managed it yet.

But a permission rule cannot tell whether the thinking stayed with the student. If students themselves cannot always tell when the thinking has left the task, a rule written for the whole school cannot tell either. The ticket generator would have passed any traffic light. AI was allowed for that project.

As Susanna Loeb, one of the review’s authors, put it to Education Week: asking whether AI is good for learning is like asking whether paper is. It depends on how you use it.

Where it is decided: the task

How a student uses AI is decided less by the tool and more by the task in front of them. A task that rewards a working output invites the shortcut. A task that makes the student’s own reasoning visible first, for example by writing the pricing rules and a few test cases by hand before any code is generated, keeps the thinking where it belongs. The tool stays in the room.

That change is a subject decision, made by the teachers who teach the lesson. The question behind it is the same in every subject: what thinking is this task aiming to develop, and what part of it must remain the student’s?
