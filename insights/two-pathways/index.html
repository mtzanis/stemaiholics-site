Insight · October 2026

Teachers asked for two things. Most AI training gives one.

In the International Baccalaureate's consultation on AI, 1,500 educators in 119 countries separated AI for their own work from teaching with AI in the room. Here is what that means for teacher training, and where overSTEMed works.

The finding

The IB has spent 2026 asking its community what schools need in order to work well with AI. Its September update reports one distinction that deserves more attention than it has had.

Teachers separated the AI skills they need for their own professional practice from what they need in the classroom, where the job is teaching students to use AI well. They asked for the two to be treated as separate pathways, and they asked for subject-level examples of the second.

The OECD and the European Commission made a matching point this year in their AI literacy framework for schools: teacher training should not focus only on tool use.

Why the first pathway is not enough

Most AI training for teachers is the first kind: tools, prompts, and time saved on planning, feedback and admin. It is useful. It also leaves the classroom question where it was. A teacher can be fluent with every tool on the list and still not know what to change in tomorrow's task.

Why it is a department's job

What to change in a task is a subject decision, made by the people who teach the subject. It is different in physics, in history and in a language class, because the thinking each task is for is different.

It is also too much to leave to each teacher alone. That is the other thing educators told the IB, and its principle on responsibility now reflects it: teachers shape how AI is used in learning, and they cannot do it alone.

Where overSTEMed works

overSTEMed works on the second pathway. A department takes one real lesson, redesigns the task together, teaches it within two weeks, and writes up what happened. The teachers who teach the lesson are the ones who redesign it.

Here is what that looks like in one lesson. A student is debugging a program. The output is working code. The thinking is forming a guess about the cause and testing it.

An assistant can produce the working code in seconds, and the student has then skipped the only part the task was for.

So the task changes. The student writes down the guess and the test first, and only then asks for help. The tool stays in the room. The thinking stays with the student.

The question behind that change is the same in every subject: what thinking is this task aiming to develop, and what part of it must remain the student's?

If your school is working on this, or is interested in working on it, start a conversation.
