AI literacy is not
AI tool use.
The OECD and the European Commission published an AI literacy framework for schools this year.
The distinction is on page five. Most AI training in schools sits on the wrong side of it.

OECD AI literacy · Slide 03
03 / 06
The teachers
One in three teachers uses AI.
Three in four say they lack the skills to teach with it.
The gap is not access to tools. It is a staffroom that was never shown how the thing works.
OECD Teaching and Learning International Survey 2024, cited in the framework
overstemed.ai
OECD AI literacy · Slide 04
04 / 06
On learning
Better work, faster.
Not more learning.
Students using AI produce better work, sooner. The framework’s reading of the evidence: the gains do not hold.
A faster finish is not a deeper understanding.
overstemed.ai
OECD AI literacy · Slide 05
05 / 06
What it asks of teacher training
Not the tools.
The teaching.
Teacher training, it says, “should not focus solely on tool use, but on appropriate teaching methods.”
In plain terms: not which buttons to press, but how a lesson changes once the tool is in the room.
Most AI training offered to schools is a tour of the tools. The framework asks for the reverse.
overstemed.ai

AI-proof · Slide 03
03 / 06
The real damage
The problem is not the student.
It is what this does to the teacher.
Tell a teacher a task is AI-proof, and the first student who gets around it makes them think the whole idea failed.
One student skipping the work is a small loss. A teacher giving up on the method is a big one.
overstemed.ai
AI-proof · Slide 04
04 / 06
A better question
Do not ask “can they cheat?”
They can.
Ask these two instead:
How much work does skipping actually save them?
Would you notice it in what they hand in?
